Why we’re building it

Most good ideas die waiting in a backlog.

Every team has a list of things that would make life better: the internal tool nobody has time for, the small fix that keeps slipping, the feature a customer asked for last spring.

The work isn’t mysterious. It’s a spec, a design, some careful code and a proper review. It just never gets to the top of the pile.

Deplx is our attempt to change that without cutting corners. It does the steps a good team does, in the same order, and it shows its work so you can trust what comes out.

How it works

Same steps a good team takes. You sign off on the big ones.

Each step is done by a specialist with one job: a product lead, an architect, a security reviewer, designers, engineers and testers. Each one hands its work to the next, and reviewers never mark their own homework.

  1. Tell it what you wantDescribe the idea in the chat, as you would to a colleague. Deplx works out what kind of work it is and confirms it back to you. A question or a chat creates nothing.
  2. Approve the planYou get a short spec with numbered acceptance criteria. Before you approve, it walks you through any open questions, each with a recommendation: take it, choose another option, write your own answer, or let the team decide.
  3. Approve the designHow it will be built, a security threat model written up front, and for anything on screen, mockups you can view full size, approve or set aside for later.
  4. Tests first, then the buildAcceptance tests are written before the code, so “done” means something. Engineers then build in their own separate copy of your project. If two pieces of work collide, you’re told, never surprised.
  5. A second pair of eyesSeparate reviewers check the design and the security, screens get a design check, and testers confirm every criterion. If a reviewer and a builder go round three times without agreeing, Deplx stops and asks you.
  6. You approve the releaseThis step is always yours, even in hands-off mode. Today a release ends with a written release record in your project; shipping straight to your cloud is still to come.

What it can take on

Not just new features.

Different work needs a different path, so a one-line bug fix doesn’t go through the same ceremony as a brand-new feature.

A new feature

The full path: plan, design and threat model, tests, build, independent review, validation, and your sign-off at the plan, the design and the release.

A bug fix

A failing test that reproduces the bug comes first, then the fix, review and validation.

A screen or two

Mockups you can react to before any code is written, then the build and a design check against what you approved.

Housekeeping

Upgrades and configuration chores, with your sign-off before anything is released.

When something breaks

It works out what went wrong first, then fixes it through the bug-fix path, test and review included.

A security sweep

A focused look across the project for weaknesses. Small concerns go on a list you can act on; real problems become fixes.

Nothing hidden

Everything it writes, you can read.

Deplx shows its work. Every plan, design and report lands in your project as a plain document, and the app shows you exactly what changed at each step.

The paperwork a good team keeps

Specs, designs, threat models, mockups, review and test reports, and a release record for every piece of work.

Changes, step by step

See the code changes each step made, side by side, and open the project in VS Code or Cursor in one click.

A full history

Every decision on a timeline, newest first, with anything Deplx decided for you clearly marked “Handled for you”.

An approvals inbox

Everything waiting on you in one place, across all your projects, with anything you set aside kept on its own list.

Plain-language escalations

When it gets stuck, it tells you what happened, what it recommends and how confident it is. You can send it back, accept, override or cancel.

What it costs

Spending per task and per project, a gauge of how much of your plan you’ve used, and an export for your records.

Two ways to work

Hands on, or hands off. You choose.

Some days you want to approve every step. Other days you want to come back to finished work. Switch whenever you like.

D

Developer mode

Every decision is yours. You also get a built-in editor with your project’s files, a helper that suggests changes, run and build commands with a problems list, a git panel for commits and branches, and code navigation for TypeScript and JavaScript.

G

Guided mode

Deplx answers questions that have a clear recommendation, approves the plan and design when no reviewer objected, waits out usage limits and picks up again, and keeps a daily summary for you. Its spending on your behalf has a daily cap that starts at zero until you raise it. It never approves a release, waives a security block or cancels work for you.

Staying in control

Built for people who don’t like surprises.

It runs on your own computer, on the projects you point it at, and nothing important happens without you.

Pause, rewind, reprioritise

Pause any piece of work with a reason, send it back to the plan or the design, move it sooner or later, or stop it.

A sensible pace

Up to three pieces of work at once by default. It slows down near your limits and never runs two tasks on the same files at the same time.

Spending limits

Limits per step and per task, and new work waits when you’re close to your plan’s limit.

Fenced in

Work happens inside a sandbox, in its own copy of your project, and writes outside a role’s assigned area are caught.

Secrets stay secret

Passwords, keys and credentials are hidden from its records, including ones tucked inside web addresses.

Only on your machine

The app is reachable only from your own computer. Nothing is hosted, and there’s no account to create.

Working today

  • Six kinds of work, each with its own path
  • Several projects side by side, with an approvals inbox across them
  • Developer and Guided modes, with a built-in editor in Developer mode

What we’re working toward

  • Starting from the tools your team already uses for tickets, docs and code
  • Learning an existing codebase, not just new projects
  • Updates where your team already talks
