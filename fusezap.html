The problem

“Can it run our model?” shouldn’t take months to answer.

A customer evaluating a new chip asks one question first: will it run the model we already use? Too often the honest answer is “not yet”.

Behind that answer is a small compiler team working flat out, porting one model at a time while two to four notable new models arrive every month. Meanwhile, the customer’s evaluation window is closing.

FuseZap exists so the answer becomes “yes, and here’s the proof”.

How it works

Two things your chip needs. We take care of both.

FuseZap sits between your chip’s own toolchain and your customers’ applications. You keep your compiler and drivers; we build everything above them, and prove it works.

C

FuseZap Core: get models running

Every model breaks down into about 28 basic operations. FuseZap generates and tunes the code for those on your chip, and calls your own tuned kernels where they’re faster. On top sits a server your customers talk to exactly the way they talk to the tools they already use, with batching, streaming and 8-bit formats built in.

V

FuseZap Validate: keep them running

Every model is checked against a trusted reference, token for token, and measured for speed and accuracy after every model, SDK or driver change. You get a qualification report your customers can read, and you hear about a regression before they do.

You bring

A way to compile a kernel, a driver to allocate memory and launch work, and a board, card or simulator. Your tuned matrix and attention kernels are welcome too.

We build

A backend for your chip in four small pieces, fused and tuned kernels for everything your libraries don’t cover, and the serving stack above them.

Before silicon

A functional simulator with the same interface is enough to start. We prove correctness there, and tune for speed once real hardware arrives.

Where we are

Right first. Fast is catching up.

In October we ran FuseZap beside vLLM, the open-source engine most teams already trust, on the same NVIDIA H100 with a 7-billion-parameter model. vLLM is still faster, and we say so. Earlier this month we were at 3% of its speed.
